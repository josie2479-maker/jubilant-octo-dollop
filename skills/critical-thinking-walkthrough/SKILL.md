# Critical Thinking Walkthrough

**Created by Josie Lagarde, designed with Claude (Cowork)** · Created: 2026-09-01

**License:** Released under CC BY 4.0 — free to use, adapt, and share with
attribution.

**Provenance:** the six-lens Who / What / Where / When / Why / How framing is
adapted from the widely-circulated "Ultimate Cheatsheet for Critical Thinking"
question poster published by the Global Digital Citizen Foundation. The lens
structure is theirs; the question banks, selection rule, and output format
below are original to this skill. The closing "smallest step" question is a
handwritten addition to the original poster and is treated here as the
required last move, not an optional extra.

---

## What this is for

Someone hands you information you did not generate — an article, a statistic,
a vendor claim, a policy change, a piece of advice, a proposal, a headline —
and you do not yet have a position on it. This skill builds one.

It is a **thinking pass, not a fact-check.** Verification is one move inside
it (the Where lens), not the whole job.

### When NOT to use it

- **The user owns the plan and wants it attacked** → that is a plan-review /
  red-team protocol. Different job: there the goal is finding failure modes in
  something already committed to.
- **The user is already split between two known options** → that is a
  structured-debate or decision protocol. This skill is for *before* you have
  positions to weigh.
- **The user wants to understand how something works** → that is a learning
  skill. Curiosity is not scepticism.
- **A single factual lookup** ("is this number right?") → just look it up. Do
  not wrap a one-minute check in a six-lens walkthrough.

If the request is genuinely two of these at once, do this pass first, then say
plainly which other protocol the follow-on needs.

## Step 0 — State the claim in one sentence

Before any lens, write the thing being examined as a single declarative
sentence, in plain language, with the hedges stripped out.

This step does more work than it looks like it does. Most weak claims fail
here: they cannot survive being written down without their qualifiers, or the
sentence turns out to contain three separate claims wearing one coat. If that
happens, split them and say which one you are examining.

Also record, in one line each:

- **Source** — who is telling you this, and how you came to see it.
- **Stake** — what changes for the user depending on whether it is true. If
  nothing changes, say so and stop; not everything deserves a walkthrough.

If the input is too thin to state as a claim, do not pad it. Say what is
missing and ask for it.

## The Six Lenses

Each lens has a question bank. **These are candidates, not a checklist.** See
the Selection Rule below — the bank exists so you can find the sharp question,
not so you can ask all of them.

### Who
- Who benefits if this is believed and acted on? Who pays?
- Who is most directly affected, and were they consulted or only described?
- Who decides — and is that the same party as the one presenting it?
- Who is *not* in this picture who obviously should be?
- Whose expertise would change your read on it, and have you heard from them?
- Who else is saying this, and are they independent of the original source or
  citing it?

### What
- What is the strongest version of the opposing case?
- What would have to be true for this to hold, and is it?
- What is the best case, the worst case, and the boring likely case?
- What is being left out — and is the omission convenient for someone?
- What is the actual claim versus the implied one? (What it says versus what
  it wants you to conclude.)
- What is the alternative explanation for the same evidence?

### Where
- Where did this come from originally — and is the chain of citation intact,
  or does everyone cite everyone else back to nothing?
- Where would this be observable in the real world, and is it?
- Where does this hold and where does it break? (Context, scale, geography,
  population.)
- Where would you go to disconfirm it, if you wanted to?
- Where are the comparable cases, and how did they turn out?

### When
- When was this true? Is it still?
- What is the time horizon — does it hold next quarter, next decade, both?
- When would you know you were wrong? Name the observable.
- Is the timing of *this being said now* itself informative?
- When does the cost of acting on it become unrecoverable?

### Why
- Why is this being said now, by this source, in this form?
- Why has it been this way for so long — is the status quo evidence, inertia,
  or someone's interest?
- Why would a smart, honest person disagree?
- Why does it feel persuasive? Separate the argument from its delivery.
- Why is this relevant to the user specifically, versus generally interesting?

### How
- How would you know the difference between this being true and false?
- How was this measured, and by whom, and what does the method exclude?
- How does this change if the scale changes — ten times bigger, ten times
  smaller?
- How does it get used in practice, by people with their own incentives?
- How is this similar to something that already went well or badly?
- How would acting on it be reversed if it turned out wrong?

## Selection Rule

The value of this skill is subtraction. A walkthrough that asks thirty
questions has done the user's thinking *for* nobody.

1. **Pick at most two questions per lens, and skip lenses that do not bite.**
   A well-run pass usually lands on six to ten questions total.
2. **A lens you skip gets one line saying why** — "Who: single-author blog
   post, no party stands to gain, skipped." Visible skips keep the pass
   honest; silent ones make it look thorough when it wasn't.
3. **Prefer the question whose answer would change the conclusion.** If
   answering it either way leaves you in the same place, it is a decorative
   question. Cut it.
4. **Answer every question you ask.** A question you cannot answer is not
   dropped — it is written down as an open question with the specific thing
   that would resolve it ("unknown — the study's sample size, which is not in
   the summary; the paper would say"). An unanswered question left as a
   rhetorical flourish is the main failure mode of this whole genre.
5. **Verify rather than reason about it, where verification is cheap.** If a
   claim's source, date, or number can be checked with a search in the time it
   takes to write a paragraph speculating about it, check it. Say plainly
   which parts you verified and which you reasoned about.
6. **Do not manufacture doubt.** If the thing holds up, the finding is "this
   holds up, here is the one soft spot." Scepticism that always concludes
   "it's complicated" is as useless as credulity.

## Output Format

```
**The claim:** [one sentence, hedges stripped]
**Source:** [who, and how it reached you]
**Stake:** [what changes depending on the answer]

**What I looked at**

| Lens | The question that mattered | What I found |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Who | ... | ... |
| What | ... | ... |
| ... | ... | ... |

*Skipped: [lens — one-line reason each]*

**Where I landed:** [2–4 sentences. An actual position — "this is probably
right, and the reason it feels shakier than it is, is X" or "the core claim
holds but the recommendation attached to it does not follow." Not a summary of
the table.]

**Still open:** [each unresolved question, with what would settle it. Omit the
section if nothing is open.]

**Smallest next step:** [one concrete action, doable this week, that either
tests the claim or acts on it at low cost. Never "do more research" — name the
specific thing to read, ask, measure, or try.]
```

Keep table cells to one to three sentences. If a lens surfaced two things, put
the sharper one in the cell and fold the second in as a trailing clause rather
than adding rows.

## Pre-flight check

Re-read your draft against these before delivering. Rules written once at the
top of a skill get violated during the flow of producing output; this catches
it while the fix is cheap.

1. Is the claim stated in **one** sentence, with hedges removed — and is it
   one claim, not three?
2. Did you ask **at most two questions per lens**, and is every skipped lens
   accounted for in one line?
3. Is **every question you asked answered**, or explicitly listed under "Still
   open" with the thing that would resolve it?
4. Did you say which parts you **verified** versus **reasoned about**? Any
   cheap verification you skipped and speculated about instead?
5. Does "Where I landed" state an actual position, rather than restating the
   table or hedging into "it depends"?
6. Is the smallest next step **concrete and small** — a specific thing to
   read, ask, measure, or try — and not "do more research"?
7. Did you distinguish what the source **says** from what it **implies**, and
   from what *you* concluded? Three different things; label them.
8. If the material holds up, did you say so plainly instead of hunting for a
   flaw to justify the exercise?

## Anti-patterns

- **The question dump.** Reproducing the whole bank with no answers. This is
  the failure this skill exists to prevent.
- **Scepticism theatre.** Concluding "there are considerations on both sides"
  when the evidence actually points one way.
- **Attacking the source instead of the claim.** Who benefits is a lens for
  finding what to check, not a substitute for checking it. A biased source can
  be right.
- **Symmetry bias.** Giving equal column-width to a strong claim and a weak
  objection because the table has two sides.
- **Answering a different, easier question.** "Is this study well-designed"
  quietly becoming "do I like this conclusion."
- **Ending without a position.** The user came in without one; if they leave
  without one, the pass failed.

## Related skills

**Escalation:** if the walkthrough ends genuinely split — two readings both
survive the six lenses and the stakes are high — that is the signal to hand
off to a structured multi-round debate protocol rather than running this pass
again harder. Running it twice does not break a real tie.

**Boundary:** a plan-review / red-team protocol handles work the user owns and
is committed to; this one handles information they have just received and do
not yet have a position on. If the user asks for both, do this first — you
cannot stress-test a plan built on a claim you have not examined.

Neither is required. This skill works on its own.
